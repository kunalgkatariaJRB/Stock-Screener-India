    House Doctrine

    The principles
    that govern this ledger.

    We do not trade. We allocate. The list above is rebuilt around these principles, drawn from those who have actually compounded capital across decades — Graham, Buffett, Munger, and Naval Ravikant.

    1. i

      Mr. Market is your servant, not your guide.

      The market quotes prices every day. Those quotes reflect emotion as much as analysis. We buy when he is depressed, we sell when he is manic, otherwise we ignore him. The portfolio is built from value, not from the tape.

      — Graham, The Intelligent Investor
    2. ii

      A wonderful business at a fair price > a fair business at a wonderful price.

      The cheapest stock is rarely the best one. Quality compounds over time; cheapness without quality is a trap. We start with return on capital, not the price tag.

      — Buffett (1989 Letter to Shareholders)
    3. iii

      Margin of safety is a habit, not a slogan.

      We require a real gap between what something is worth and what it costs. Estimates are imperfect; the gap protects us from our imperfections. When the gap closes, conviction must be earned again.

      — Graham, central thesis of value investing
    4. iv

      Be fearful when others are greedy, and greedy when others are fearful.

      The biggest gains in investing come from buying against a gloomy crowd, on the upside. Crowd sentiment is a contrary indicator at extremes — the low price already bakes in their pessimism. Losses are capped at 1×; gains are uncapped.

      — Buffett · refined by Naval ("non-consensus and right")
    5. v

      Time horizons are measured in cycles, not quarters.

      "Our favourite holding period is forever." Compounding is the only force that turns small bets into large outcomes — and it requires sitting still through quarters that look ugly. Three to five years is the minimum for any thesis we believe in.

      — Buffett · echoed by Naval ("all returns come from compound interest")
    6. vi

      Play long-term games with long-term people.

      Trust, reputation, and compounding all benefit from continuity. This applies to companies you own as much as to people you invest alongside. A great management team is almost always a multi-decade hold.

      — Naval Ravikant
    7. vii

      Stay inside your circle of competence.

      You don't have to be right about everything — only about the companies you own. Investing in businesses you don't understand is gambling, however sophisticated the wrapper. Our verdicts here are confined to industries we can read.

      — Buffett & Munger
    8. viii

      Concentration with conviction; diversification against ignorance.

      Six to ten ideas you understand deeply will outperform forty you barely follow. But diversify across sectors, not within one. A portfolio that is over-concentrated in any single industry is one bad cycle from impairment.

      — Buffett's portfolio in practice
    9. ix

      Sell when the thesis breaks — not when the price falls.

      A 30% drawdown on a healthy compounder is a chance to add. A 30% drawdown on a broken story is a warning. The price is a question; the fundamentals are the answer.

      — Munger's discipline
    10. x

      Position size is the only real risk control.

      No single bet, however brilliant, justifies a position that could materially impair the family's capital. Size for the worst case, not the best. Survival precedes return — always.

      — Taleb's first lesson, echoed by Naval
